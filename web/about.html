About the project

Test Mars drills where the ground behaves like Mars

Every drill bound for Mars is tested on Earth first. Planetary Twin finds the places on Earth whose ground best matches a chosen spot on Mars, at the depth the drill will actually reach, and explains why.

How it works

  1. 1

    Pick a spot and a depth

    Anywhere within ±70° latitude on Mars, snapped to a 2° grid (about 120 km), at one of four depths: 0–10 cm, 10 cm–1 m, 1–3 m, or deeper than 3 m.

  2. 2

    Describe the ground

    For that depth we estimate temperature, how much it swings, how much of the year it stays frozen, ground ice, ground type, hardness, rock type, slope and roughness.

  3. 3

    Compare with Earth

    The same ten features are computed for bare ground on Earth. Each Earth place gets a distance from the Mars spot, and the closest ones become its twins.

  4. 4

    Explain the result

    Every twin comes with a match percentage, the features that agree and differ, and a confidence level based on how much of the data was measured rather than modelled.

The maths, in plain English

There are five steps, and none of them is a black box.

AWorking out conditions underground

Nobody has measured the temperature 2 m below most of Mars, so we calculate it. The surface warms by day and cools by night, and warms in summer and cools in winter. Those swings travel down into the ground but shrink quickly: every extra skin depth cuts the swing to about a third of what it was above.

swing at depth = swing at surface × e−depth / skin depthskin depth = √(heat diffusivity × cycle length / π)

How fast heat moves through the ground comes from its thermal inertia, which orbiters measure from how quickly the surface heats up. For typical Martian soil the daily swing fades over about 4 cm and the yearly swing over about a metre. Below that, the ground sits close to its yearly average temperature. The same model runs on Earth, so both planets are treated the same way.

BPutting two planets on the same scale

Mars is 60–100 °C colder than Earth, so comparing raw temperatures would match nothing. Instead each place is ranked within its own planet: a spot in the coldest 10% of Mars is compared with places in the coldest 10% of Earth.

  • Ranked (percentile): temperature, temperature swing, dryness.
  • Same or different: ground ice, ground type, rock type.
  • Steps: hardness, from 0 (loose soil) to 3 (hard bedrock).
  • Logarithmic: slope and roughness. Going from 1° to 2° matters more than going from 20° to 21°.

CMeasuring how different two places are

For each feature we score the difference between 0 (identical) and 1 (as different as possible). The overall distance is a weighted average of those scores, a method known as the Gower distance. Features that matter most for drilling, like ground type and hardness, carry more weight. Features that are missing on either side are skipped rather than guessed.

DTurning distance into a match percentage

A distance of 0.22 is hard to read, so it becomes a percentage on a curve that falls off smoothly:

match % = 100 × e−distance / ss is set so that a typical best match on Mars scores about 75%

With s ≈ 0.56, the example above scores 67.5%. The percentage is relative: 75% means "as good as a typical best match", not "75% identical". The labels are excellent (85+), good (70+), fair (50+), poor (30+) and no match. If less than half of the weighted features could be compared, the result shows "not enough data" instead of a number.

EPicking the twins and setting confidence

Earth places are sorted by distance and the closest are kept, as long as each one is at least 200 km from the twins already picked, so the list is not five neighbouring valleys. Two further rules apply:

  • Ice rule. If the Mars spot has no ground ice at any depth, Earth sites with permanent ground ice or snow cover are left out, even when other features line up.
  • Confidence. High when most values were measured. Lower when they came from the heat model or the Earth gap-filling model (a gradient-boosted classifier that predicts ground ice and ground type where maps have gaps), and lower again for the deepest layer.

Untested sites

Most Mars analog work happens in a short list of famous places: the Atacama, Devon Island, the Dry Valleys, Rio Tinto, Svalbard, Utah. Planetary Twin also scores bare ground across the rest of Earth. Places outside those known analog regions are marked Untested. The explorer lists them even when they fall a few points below the main cut-off, because a slightly weaker match nobody has tested may be more useful than another visit to a well-worn site.

Data

Mars

  • MOLA elevation, 463 m (slope, roughness)
  • TES thermal inertia (heat model, ground type)
  • Mars Odyssey neutron hydrogen (ground ice)
  • USGS geologic map SIM 3292 (rock type)
  • Mars Climate Database (dryness)
  • Imagery: CTX global mosaic (~5 m/pixel), Viking MDIM 2.1 colour

Earth

  • ERA5-Land soil temperature, MODIS land surface temperature
  • ESA CCI permafrost, NSIDC circum-Arctic ground ice
  • SoilGrids texture and density, GLiM lithology
  • Copernicus DEM, ESA WorldCover (bare ground)
  • Global Aridity Index
  • Imagery: Esri World Imagery

Limits

Matches are research leads for choosing field-test sites, not drilling safety certifications. Subsurface values on both planets are partly modelled, the Mars grid is coarse (2°), and confidence falls with depth. Rock classes come from geologic maps, not mineral spectra, so the app cannot yet point to specific clays or sulfates.
